It is important to distinguish between the Gibbs energy of reaction, Δr G and the standard state Gibbs energy of reaction, Δr G°. The ° refers to standard state conditions. That is, each reactant and product has a partial pressure of 1 bar if a gas, a concentration of 1 M if a solution, and they are all unmixed from each other. Such idealized conditions, while convenient for serving as a reference state, do not actually represent real reaction conditions. Consider the reaction of nitrogen with hydrogen to form ammonia:

N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g)

Δr G° = −32.9 kJ/mol

[Note: corrected; the source prints "−32.9 (kJ)/(mol K)" where this reads "−32.9 kJ/mol". A Gibbs energy of reaction is in kJ/mol (2 × −16.45 kJ/mol for NH3).]

If the reaction was run under standard state conditions (1 bar partial pressure of each gas), the reaction would shift towards the products since Δr G° < 0. That is, the partial pressures of N2 and H2 will decrease and the partial pressure of NH3 will increase until equilibrium is reached. The Gibbs energy of reaction is dependent on the composition:

[Note: corrected; the source prints "N2 and O2" where this reads "N2 and H2". The reactants are N2 and H2.]

Δr G = Δr G° + RT ln Q = Δr G° + RT ln((P_NH3^2)/(P_N2 P_H2^3))

At equilibrium, the minimum Gibbs energy will be reached, where the Gibbs energy of reaction is zero:

[Note: corrected; the source prints "At equilibrium, the minimum Gibbs energy of reaction will be reached:" where this reads "At equilibrium, the minimum Gibbs energy will be reached, where the Gibbs energy of reaction is zero:". G has its minimum at equilibrium; ΔrG, its slope, is zero there.]

Δr G = 0 kJ/mol

[Note: corrected; the source prints "0 (kJ)/(mol K)" where this reads "0 kJ/mol". As above: kJ/mol.]

And the reaction quotient will equal the equilibrium constant:

Q = K
